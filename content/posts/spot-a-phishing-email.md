Phishing is still one of the most common ways accounts get compromised, because it targets people, not software. The good news: a short routine catches most of it.

## The 30-second check

1. **Look at the sender address, not just the name.** `Support <help@paypa1-security.com>` is not PayPal. Check the domain after the @ sign.
2. **Notice the pressure.** "Your account will be closed in 24 hours" is designed to make you act before you think.
3. **Hover before you click.** On a computer, hover over a link to preview the real destination. On a phone, press and hold the link.
4. **Question unexpected attachments.** Invoices, voicemails and "shared documents" you did not expect deserve suspicion.
5. **Watch for odd requests.** Gift cards, urgent wire transfers, or "just reply with your password" are red flags.
6. **Go around the message.** Do not use the link. Open the company's site or app yourself, or call the number on your card.

## Common disguises

| Disguise | What it really wants |
| --- | --- |
| Delivery notice | Your card details for a "redelivery fee" |
| Boss asking for a favor | Gift cards or a quick payment |
| Shared document | Your work login |
| Security alert | Your password and verification code |

## If you already clicked

- Do not enter anything further. Close the page.
- If you typed a password, change it right away on the real site, and anywhere you reused it.
- Turn on multi-factor authentication for important accounts.
- Tell your IT or security team quickly. Reporting fast matters more than being embarrassed.

## Make it a habit

Use a password manager. It will not autofill on a fake site, which is a surprisingly good warning sign. And when in doubt, slow down: real organizations can wait five minutes.
